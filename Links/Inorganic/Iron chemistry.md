The Rusting Process
requires the presence of both dioxygen and water.

On iron surface: O2(g) + 2H2O (I) + 4 e- $\rightarrow$ 4OH- (aq) increase the pH

The bulk iron acts like a wire connected to a battery, conveying electrons from
another point on the surface that has a lower oxygen concentration, a point at
which the iron metal is oxidized to iron(II) ions: 
Fe(s) $\rightarrow$ Fe2+(aq) + 2e-

These two ions diffuse through the solution, and where they meet, insoluble
iron(II) hydroxide is formed:

Fe2+(aq) + 2 OH-(aq) $\rightarrow$ Fe(OH)2 (s).

And thus:

Fe(OH)2(s) + ½O2 (aq) + e- $\rightarrow$ FeO(OH)(s) + OH- (l).

Iron Oxides
There are three common oxides of iron:

The iron(II) oxide is basic, dissolving in acids: FeO(s) + 2H+ (aq) $\rightarrow$ Fe2+(aq) + H2O(l)

Iron(III) oxide, or hematite, is found in large underground deposits, the oldest being
about 2 billion years old and can exist in different crystalline forms.

Magnetite, Fe3O4, has commonly inverse spinel structure and is ferrimagnetic
pieces of this magnetic compound were used as a primitive compass.

## Ferrites
mixed-metal oxides, one metal being iron (III), often have spinel structures

“soft” ferrites: can be magnetized rapidly and efficiently by an electromagnet, but they lose their magnetism as soon as the current is discontinued.

Structure: MFe2O4, M = Mn2+, Ni2+, Co2+, or Mg2+

Applications: Transformers and Antennas “hard” ferrites: the hard ferrites retain their magnetic properties constantly

Structure: MFe12O19, M = Ba2+, Sr2+

Applications: permanent magnets, e,g. refrigerator magnets.
